Two-Cord Static Equilibrium – 7 kg Block

Problem Statement

A 7 kg block hangs from a ring that is supported by two cords. The left cord makes a 60° angle with the horizontal bar, and the right cord makes a 40° angle. The system is at rest. Determine the tension in each cord. Provide a full reasoning process, include diagrams or animations that clarify the geometry, and explain how to verify the result numerically.

Given data:

  • Mass of the block: m = 7 kg
  • Acceleration due to gravity: g = 9.81 m/s² (adjustable)
  • Left cord angle: 60° from the horizontal
  • Right cord angle: 40° from the horizontal

Guided Solution Tabs

Mindset Kickoff

Picture the node at the junction of the two cords and the vertical link to the block. The node is motionless, so the vector sum of all forces on it must vanish. Each cord transmits tension along its own direction, and the weight vector pulls straight down.

Extract the Clues
  • Known mass fixes the weight: W = mg.
  • Angles are measured from the horizontal bar, so cosine controls horizontal pull and sine controls vertical lift.
  • Equilibrium at the node means ΣFx = 0 and ΣFy = 0.
  • Two unknowns (tension T₁ and T₂) pair with two linear equations.

Partial-Credit Strategy
  • Identify forces: draw the free-body diagram and label components to secure modeling points.
  • Write balance equations: even without solving, writing ΣFx = 0 and ΣFy = 0 earns procedural credit.
  • Substitute numbers carefully: plug in W = 7 × 9.81 to gather calculation marks.
  • Check magnitudes: compare against weight to argue which cord bears more load.

Goal: express horizontal and vertical balance at the node.

Horizontal Balance

ΣFx = 0 ⇒ T₁ cos θ₁ = T₂ cos θ₂

The horizontal pull of the left cord is canceled by that of the right cord. This equation alone links the two tensions.

Vertical Balance

ΣFy = 0 ⇒ T₁ sin θ₁ + T₂ sin θ₂ = W

The upward components of both tensions must support the weight W = mg.

Why Angles from the Horizontal?
  • The geometry in the figure references the bar, so cosine projects along the bar and sine projects vertically.
  • Either choice (from the horizontal or vertical) works as long as it is applied consistently.

Goal: solve the two linear equations for T₁ and T₂.

Substitution Route

T₁ = T₂ · (cos θ₂ / cos θ₁)

[(cos θ₂ / cos θ₁) sin θ₁ + sin θ₂] · T₂ = W

T₂ = W · cos θ₁ / sin(θ₁ + θ₂)

T₁ = W · cos θ₂ / sin(θ₁ + θ₂)

The denominator simplifies elegantly because sin(θ₁ + θ₂) = sin θ₁ cos θ₂ + cos θ₁ sin θ₂.

Symbolic Observations
  • If θ₁ increases (steeper left cord), cos θ₂ stays the same, so T₁ drops—more vertical alignment means less tension needed.
  • The cord closer to horizontal always carries more load because its vertical component is small.
  • The sum θ₁ + θ₂ controls the denominator; if the two cords become nearly colinear, tensions skyrocket.

Knowledge Wrap-Up

  • Static equilibrium: both ΣFx and ΣFy must vanish at the node.
  • Component reasoning: angles from the horizontal keep cosine tied to horizontal pulls.
  • System sensitivities: tensions rise quickly if either cord approaches the horizontal.
  • Transferable skill: the same algebra works for suspension bridges, camera rigs, and crane slings.
Key Exam Targets
  • Concept: interpreting the free-body diagram correctly.
  • Technique: resolving forces with sine/cosine relative to the chosen reference.
  • Computation: handling the algebra without sign slips.
  • Communication: stating why the larger tension sits on the smaller angle cord.
Common Pitfalls
  • Mixing up sine and cosine because angles are given from the horizontal.
  • Forgetting that both tensions help support the weight.
  • Leaving answers as forces without units.
Extension Ideas
  • Introduce a third cord or a horizontal push to explore statically indeterminate setups.
  • Let g vary to simulate other planets or elevator acceleration.
  • Attach a second mass on the right cord and analyze coupled tensions.
